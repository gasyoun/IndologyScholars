## 1. Радим Навьян — 2014-10-29 03:03:41

Я порылся в архивах сообщества и увидел, что здесь есть специалисты по 
Восьмикнижию. 

В этой связи у меня простой вопрос: в каком месте текста Панини излагает 
правила и стандарты письменной речи санскрита? 
В частности, мне важно знать, где содержатся предписания для
а) слитного написания слова с началом на гласную и предыдущего слова 
(अत्र+अस्ति), 
б) слитного написания слова с исходом на закрытый слог и следующего за ним 
слова (तत्+फलम्)‍, 
в) наконец, отражения на письме незабвенных правил сандхи (кои суть - 
объективно присутствующие фонетические изменения устной речи, а вовсе не 
правила).

Если этих правил нет у Панини, тогда кто из более поздних грамматистов их 
ввёл? Если никто из грамматистов не вводил, тогда какой умник ответственен 
за то, что санскрит такой корявый и неудобный для чтения с листа? Ну 
хорошо, на последний вопрос я сам найду ответ. Но все остальные в силе.

РН

## 2. Gaurav Shorey — 2014-10-29 06:28:03

Traditionally, Sanskrit wasn't a written language.

The written script was only introduced about 1500 years back. Till then it
was auditory and transferred only via word of mouth. Yes, even tomes like
Mahabharata were transferred orally and aurally. So there is no way rules
to "written" Sanskrit can be found.

So there will always be a gap between what Is written and what is
interpreted by us since the authors experiences do not translate to paper.

What the west is now discovering (that the language symbolizes a
representation / interpretation of the event / occurring and not the event
/ occurring itself, which may not be represented linguistically).

See works of korzibsky in general semantics for further details on this.

best

gaurava

## 4. Gaurav Shorey — 2014-10-29 08:56:54

Yes. Thanks.

Like it says in arthashastra of kautilya - "the atom is the smallest
particle that is visible to the naked eye by a person who has stilled the
mind"

Stilling the mind was the focus, oral transmission and the ability to see
the atom with the naked eye was the natural outcome.

It appears impossible to me today, that is mostly because I was not taught
to listen, but more to judge, evaluate, disagree or agree or look for whats
right and whats wrong. When people speak I am constantly trying to prove my
point - I am trying to do that right now as well if you notice - so rote
memorizing and oral transmission in Sanskrit seem impossible to me.

What is mentioned in the book you sent (I have read it) is the case
presented by a person to whom it seems impossible that all this  great,
ancient civilization created, in terms of art, literature, law, grammar,
etc. would have remotely been possible without language.

That is impassioned conjecture - otherwise Kautilya too would have
mentioned that "the smallest particle can be seen via glass molded into a
certain shape, put together in what is commonly referred to as a
microscope".

Now, In response,

please read any of the books By Arthur Berridale Keith on archive.org

or

https://archive.org/details/ahistoryofindian12956gut

or

http://www.amazon.com/Veda-Recitation-Varanasi-Wayne-Howard/dp/8120800710

(which is now out of print but I have one surviving copy)

or

http://www.svbf.org/journal/vol1no2/chanting.pdf

or

http://web.unitn.it/files/download/24457/hartzell.pdf  on the process of
memorizing the accents that supported chanting the vedas and its impact on
the physiology.



I am stressing on the transmission of the vedas primarily and then texts
that came after that - since the Rg veda is considered the oldest
extant composition in the Indo-european language world (
http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigveda).

There is no end to the number of western scholars who have, via
research, suggested the oral traditions of transmitting education and the
presence of script "as a mere tool to nudge scholars" forward - so no
exhaustive stress on the written word.

My memory fails me at this moment but I recall reading somewhere that for
12 years, brahmacharya involved studying grammar, to master the spoken word
-  I don't recall them reading it via script.

Additionally, since we are so bound by the written script ourselves, a
world outside ours seems impossible too.

Have you noticed how we can read "in our minds?" Have you tried reading
Sanskrit in the mind? Its next to impossible, since some syllables must be
vocalized (may be under your breath) to be spoken.

The reverse may have been the origin - couldn't it?



Then again, everything I present is merely a point of view and not the
truth.

## 5. ShaSa — 2014-10-29 13:47:28

Namaste
Writing is as old as oral tradition if not older.. Lack of written records 
alone cannot be taken as evidence for non-existence of writing systems. Why 
else would Goddess sarasvatI be praised as holding book in hand, if there 
existed no writing systems?? 
Heard from elders that- in India, both systems developed independent of 
each other. 
Audio aid is better than video- may be it was felt so by ancient people.. 
and hence followed more. I am not aware of any research in this area. May 
be ancients knew that sound played a better role than sight in learning.
Reason for lack of punctuation in ancient books *as per my understanding*-
First, developing palm-leaf-books (for eg.) was very time taking, tough and 
expenditure involving process - scribes demanded money. So no question of 
writing as we do today in KG classes. Treating a palm leaf (or a *bhUrja 
patra*) and making it ready for a book was a very difficult affair. 
(Introduction to Manuscriptology- KS Sharma, if I remember right)
Second, space was a very important issue- they wanted to use as much of the 
leaf as possible. And keeping the book slim was an objective. They tried to 
save as much space as possible. So no word-spacing.
Third, writing was done by dictation. So scribe had to think about where to 
break the word. (So it was left out, may be.)
They gave importance to correct spelling and legible hand. 
And fourth, learning was always from a live guru than from books. So may be 
not much was cared for developing writing systems; as books were meant for 
just reference.
Hope this helps.

## 7. Radim Navyan — 2014-10-29 15:22:34

I'm not a fan of any chronology. 1500 years or 150000 years seemd to 
have no difference for human being, whose life longs for about 30-60 years.
But when we talk aboun pure ideas, the ideas are immortal. And we may 
treat to any idea as if it was born yesterday. In this case we may 
consider the degree of usefullness in any particular idea. That's what 
I'm doing with samskrta lipi.
What do I need? I need sharp sword to cut off burdens of my mind. I 
begin studying sanskrit commentary literature. The ideas in that 
commentaries are brilliant. But the outer shape of fixing is dull, not 
sharp. I spent huge amount of time not for studying, but for solving 
jigsaws of  indigestible manner of writing.
That's what I'm talking about.
Do we have any choice? Yes, we do. Panini himself gives us several options.
Let's see in sanskrit textbooks for beginners. There is totally 
different mode of writing in them. Why? Because if you try to begin your 
study with hardcore (sa-sandhi, without spaces inbetween) manner of 
writing, you'll never become a sanskrit reader. Especially if you are 
russian :)
That's my observation.
